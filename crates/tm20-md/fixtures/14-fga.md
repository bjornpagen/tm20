# Functional geometric algebra

A lesson on James Pustejovsky’s 2026 argument that Clifford algebra is a better substrate for natural language meaning than the flat vector spaces we train today. The paper is [Toward a Functional Geometric Algebra for Natural Language Semantics](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25902). This is a tape-sized reading, not a substitute for the paper.

Distributional and neural semantics have been built almost exclusively on ordinary linear algebra: vectors, matrices, tensors, addition, inner products, and learned linear maps. That toolkit retrieved well, contextualized well, and scaled into large language models. It is weakly structured when the question turns from similarity to *composition*.

## What linear algebra leaves out

Four limits sit in the algebra itself, not in any one embedding recipe.

- Weak composition. Adding vectors, concatenating them, or multiplying by a learned matrix does not say whether a verb takes a subject, an adjective modifies a noun, or a quantifier scopes over a predicate. The mode of combination is induced from data.
- Parameter inflation. Ternary relations, event frames, and scope need deeper nets or extra tensors. The interaction lives in weights, not in the algebra.
- No native types. Entities, predicates, propositions, events, and modifiers differ in kind. A shared \(R^{n}\) does not keep those kinds apart.
- A hard join with symbolic structure. Formal semantics has rules, binding, and inference over discrete objects. Distributional models have continuous similarity. Linear algebra does not mediate.

Contextual models did not remove those limits. BERT and GPT still store meaning as vectors and compose with matrix multiplies and attention. Contextualization unfreezes the word. It does not give the space a notion of binding, type, oriented composition, or recoverable structure.

> Meaning has been compositional since Frege. The algebra we train on has not.

## The geometric product

For vectors \(a\) and \(b\), the geometric product keeps two pieces that linear algebra usually splits:

\[
ab = a \cdot b + a \wedge b
\]

The inner product \(a \cdot b\) is the familiar scalar: how well two meanings align. Cosine, attention scores, retrieval. It is also lossy. Two vectors can share a dot product with a third and still point differently.

The wedge \(a \wedge b\) is antisymmetric: \(a \wedge b = -b \wedge a\). It is the oriented plane they span. Magnitude is area; sign is order. Parallel vectors wedge to zero; orthogonal vectors wedge maximally. That is the directional information the dot product throws away.

The product \(ab\) is not a pair or a concatenation. It is one element of an algebra in which those parts multiply. Addition across grades is a direct sum, not the noisy bundling of hyperdimensional computing. A scalar, a vector, and a bivector occupy orthogonal subspaces. Grade projection \(\langle M\rangle_{k}\) extracts one of them without blur.

## Clifford algebra, briefly

Let \(V\) be a real vector space with quadratic form \(Q\). The Clifford algebra \(Cl(V,Q)\) is generated by \(V\) with

\[
v^{2} = Q(v)
\]

for every vector \(v\). Squaring a vector yields a scalar: self-similarity of a direction. Distinct orthonormal generators anticommute. Anticommutativity is orientation. Together they generate the whole algebra.

The signature \((p,q,r)\) counts generators that square to \(+1\), \(-1\), and \(0\). That is a design parameter, not a footnote. Positive-squaring directions can carry extensional, similarity-preserving features; negative-squaring directions can carry intensional or contrastive ones; null generators support projective constructions. Mixed signature changes how bivectors square, and therefore whether the rotors they generate are elliptic (cyclic) or hyperbolic (boosts). The paper maps that distinction onto extensional versus intensional modification.

Quaternions are a small even subalgebra that rotates 3-space. Functional geometric algebra uses the full graded algebra: contraction, wedge, grade projection, exact unbinding. Quaternions are a special case, not the framework.

## Grades as types

Any multivector decomposes uniquely by grade. For four basis vectors the algebra has sixteen basis blades. A three-column sketch of the correspondence:

| Grade | Object | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | scalar | truth, salience |
| 1 | vector | entity, unary predicate |
| 2 | bivector | binary relation, role bind |
| 3 | trivector | event frame |
| n | pseudoscalar | discourse orientation |

The grade of a product is determined by the grades of the inputs and the operation. Contracting a grade-2 relation with a grade-1 entity yields a grade-1 unary predicate, the algebraic image of saturating one argument. That is type bookkeeping the algebra does by itself.

Entities and unary predicates can both live at grade 1, distinguished by subspace support: a direct sum \(V_{E} \oplus V_{P}\) so entity components and predicate-content components do not contaminate each other. Arity is grade: an \(n\)-place predicate needs \(n\) contractions to reach a scalar.

This is proposed as inductive bias, not as a promise that trained coefficients will sit in neat grade bins. Learned geometry is messy. The bet is that grade-sensitive operations still beat a flat vector of the same size on compositional generalization.

## Four operations

Inner product. \(\langle ab\rangle_{0}\) is scalar similarity, and full application of a unary predicate to its argument.

Wedge. For vectors, \(\langle ab\rangle_{2}\). Role–filler binding: \(r \wedge f\) binds a role to a filler. Antisymmetry distinguishes them. Bounded grade, not an ever-growing tensor.

Left and right contraction. Each reduces grade. They are not the same. For a binary relation, right-contracting with the object saturates one slot; left-contracting with the subject saturates the other. Argument direction lives in the algebra.

Rotor sandwich. \(x' = R x \tilde{R}\) for a rotor \(R\) with \(R\tilde{R} = 1\). Grade-preserving, norm-preserving, invertible, composable. Coercion and contextual modulation: rotate within a grade without changing ontological category. *Begin the book* turning a book toward its telic reading is a rotor, not a special-case rewrite.

Unbinding is exact: the inverse contraction recovers the filler. Linear-algebra tensor binding needs SVD or cleanup to approximate that.

## Inner and outer application

Outer application is classical function-argument composition: contract, reduce grade. Inner application is the default in language. *Wipe the table* wants a surface. *Tie your shoe* wants laces. *Begin the book* wants a function, usually reading. *Good knife* evaluates cutting, not an absolute scale.

Inner application is two steps: a quale-targeting rotor foregrounds a subspace of the argument, then contraction saturates. When the rotor is identity, inner application is outer application. Coercion is not a patch for type clash. It is inner application with a cross-subspace rotor.

- Outer: *exist*, *disappear*, *arrive* — the whole argument.
- Inner, same subspace: *wipe*, *drink*, *tie* — a part or surface.
- Inner, across subspaces: *begin the book*, *good knife* — telic or agentive quale.

Most composition is rotor then contraction. The paper works this through verb–argument structure, qualia-sensitive modification, and complement coercion, and shows where a flat map has to fake the distinction.

## Why 2^n is not a blow-up

A Clifford algebra on \(n\) generators has \(2^{n}\) basis elements. That looks like parameter death. The counter is structure. Pairwise and triple interactions are not free tensors; they are products the algebra already knows. Grade-truncated representations keep grades 0 through \(k\) and drop the rest. For modest \(n\) and \(k=2\), one object holds entities, predicates, and binary relations. Binomial growth \(O(n^{k}/k!)\) against a rank-\(k\) tensor \(O(n^{k})\).

The coefficients at each grade can still be learned. The *composition* of two multivectors is constrained. Savings show up at compose time.

## Functional geometric algebra

FGA is the typed, three-layer reading of this algebra for semantics: lexical meaning, compositional structure, and contextual modulation in one object. Lexical entries are multivectors whose grades match their types. Proper names are unit vectors in \(V_{E}\). Intransitives and common nouns are grade 1 with support in \(V_{E}\) and \(V_{P}\). Transitives are oriented bivectors: *John loves Mary* is not *Mary loves John* because the two contractions are not symmetric.

The paper then places this next to existing neural machinery. Attention scores already look like inner products. Multi-head mixing already looks like combining several planes. Rotations already appear as a way to move features without leaving a subspace. The claim is not that transformers secretly *are* Clifford networks. It is that the operations FGA wants are already implicit, and making them explicit would give type-sensitive learning a place to sit.

Prior art is acknowledged: subspace lattices for word sense, rotors over LSA, multivector skip-grams, geometric products in knowledge-graph embeddings, GATr and Versor as Clifford transformers in neighboring domains. FGA’s move is from encoding tricks to a compositional semantics with application, role binding, and coercion.

## What to take from the tape

Linear algebra gave us similarity and scale. It did not give us a native account of how meanings combine, or of the types those combinations must respect. Geometric algebra puts similarity and structure in one product, grades in one object, and coercion in an invertible sandwich. Whether learned multivectors will honor that scaffold is an empirical question. The algebra is already there.

Read the paper for the derivations, the three compositional sketches, the comparison with other compositional frameworks, and the path toward multivector parameterization. This lesson is the claim and the operations, set so they fit a receipt.
